Methodology

Algorithms, conventions, and sources used in kalkal

Converter Calendars Methodology

kalkal converts dates between eight historical and liturgical calendar systems using a unified algorithmic approach. All conversions are deterministic and reproducible.

Julian Day Number as Universal Pivot

All date conversions in kalkal pass through the Julian Day Number (JDN) — a continuous count of days since the beginning of the Julian Period on 1 January 4713 BCE (Julian proleptic calendar). Each calendar system implements two functions: one to convert a date into a JDN, and one to convert a JDN back into a date in that calendar. To convert from calendar A to calendar B, we compute A → JDN → B. This approach avoids the combinatorial explosion of writing direct conversion functions between every pair of calendars.

The Julian Day Number is a tool of astronomical chronology unrelated to the Julian calendar, despite the shared name. It was introduced by Joseph Justus Scaliger in 1583.

Tabular Islamic Calendar

The Hijri (lunar) calendar in kalkal uses the tabular/arithmetic method rather than astronomical observation or calculation of lunar phases. The tabular method employs a fixed 30-year cycle in which 11 years are leap years (355 days) and 19 are common years (354 days). This gives a mean lunation of 29.53̅ days, very close to the astronomical mean synodic month of 29.53059 days.

Four historical variants of the tabular calendar are supported, differing only in which years of the 30-year cycle are designated as leap. The default variant is Wüstenfeld (type IIc), the standard in Western historiography.

The tabular calendar may differ by ±1–2 days from calendars based on lunar observation (used officially in Saudi Arabia and other countries). It is intended for historical research and approximate conversion, not for determining religious observance dates.

Solar Hijri: 2820-Year Cycle

The Solar Hijri (Iranian) calendar is approximated arithmetically using a 2820-year grand cycle containing exactly 683 leap years and 1,029,983 days. This gives a mean year of 365.24220 days, extremely close to the current vernal equinox year. The algorithm, based on work by Ahmad Birashk and refined by K. M. Borkowski, determines leap years through a formula involving the cycle position.

✦

Coptic and Ethiopian: Alexandrian Calendars

Both the Coptic and Ethiopian calendars follow the Alexandrian calendar structure: 12 months of 30 days plus a short 13th month. They share the same leap year rule (year mod 4 = 3) and differ only in their epoch. Conversion to and from JDN uses straightforward arithmetic based on the fixed year length of 365.25 mean days.

✦

Yazdgerdi and Armenian: Wandering Calendars

The Yazdgerdi and Armenian calendars are "wandering" or "vague" calendars with a fixed year of exactly 365 days and no intercalation. Conversion is simple arithmetic: the JDN offset from the epoch divided by 365 gives the year, and the remainder gives the day of the year. These calendars drift through the seasons at ~1 day every 4 years.
